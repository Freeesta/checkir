## molecule

GHB (gamma-hydroxybutyric acid, 4-hydroxybutanoic acid). Free acid: C4H8O3, 104.10 g/mol, CAS 591-81-1. Identity checked on PubChem. [f:pubchem] The SWGDRUG monograph describes the sodium salt (sodium oxybate, CAS 502-85-2). [f:swgdrug-ghb]

In water GHB, its anion and gamma-butyrolactone (GBL) can all be present, in an equilibrium that depends on pH; the free acid turns into GBL when overheated. [f:swgdrug-ghb]

Bands of the sodium salt (ATR): O-H at 3325 cm-1, carboxylate bands at 1554 and 1407 cm-1 (the strongest is 1554), then 1067, 1015, 921 and 549 cm-1; the free acid instead shows the C=O at about 1702-1709 cm-1 and a very broad O-H band of a hydrogen-bonded carboxylic acid. [f:swgdrug-ghb]

The monograph notes that GHB salts often give a poor spectrum, with broad features from poorly crystallised solid and residual moisture; drying at 100 °C for a few minutes usually improves it. [f:swgdrug-ghb]

## category

Chemical class: other substances. Expected effect: depressant/sedative. Typical role in samples: active principle. This classification is a checkir proposal awaiting review. [f:unodc-ewa] [f:checkir-classification]

## history

Modern interest in GHB began in the 1960s with the physician Henri Laborit, who studied it as a GABA analogue that crosses the blood-brain barrier; it was tried as an intravenous anaesthetic (Somsanit in Germany) and abandoned in favour of agents with fewer risks. [f:busardo2015]

Recreational use spread in the 1980s-1990s, with bodybuilders using it for its apparent anabolic effect. [f:tay2022]

Sodium oxybate is registered for narcolepsy-associated cataplexy and, in some countries such as Italy and Austria, as an aid in alcohol withdrawal. [f:busardo2015]

## origin

GHB is a synthesised compound that also occurs naturally in the mammalian brain, where it derives from GABA. [f:wong2004]

GBL is a precursor in the manufacture of GHB and is converted into it in the body; aqueous samples of GHB may also contain GBL. [f:swgdrug-gbl] [f:swgdrug-ghb]

## metabolism

About 95-98 % of a dose is metabolised in the liver: GHB dehydrogenase oxidises it to succinic semialdehyde, then to succinic acid, which enters the Krebs cycle and ends as carbon dioxide and water. [f:busardo2015]

Only about 1-5 % of the dose is recovered in urine, for 3-10 h. [f:busardo2015]

Plasma half-life is 30-50 min; in 32 volunteers it was 36 ± 9 and 39 ± 7 min after 25 and 35 mg/kg by mouth. [f:busardo2015] [f:liechti2016]

Absorption and elimination are capacity-limited, so exposure rises more than in proportion to dose (AUC +40 % at the higher of two doses). [f:palatini1993] [f:liechti2016]

## effects

GHB acts as a neurotransmitter or neuromodulator in the GABAergic system, mainly on the GABA-B receptor, unlike ethanol and benzodiazepines, which act on GABA-A. [f:busardo2015]

Effects start 15-30 min after ingestion and last up to 1-6 h depending on the dose; the dose-effect curve is steep. [f:busardo2015]

At 25 and 35 mg/kg by mouth in 32 volunteers GHB gave mixed stimulant and sedative effects, with sedation and dizziness rising with dose, no change in heart rate or blood pressure and no acute tolerance. [f:liechti2016]

## doses

The UBC guidelines give 0.75-1.5 g and say that above 2.5 g the passage from waking to sleep can be sudden; a review reports 1-2 g as often enough for euphoria and a margin of only 5:1 to 8:1 between a recreational and a fatal dose. [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines] [f:busardo2015]

## risks

Poisoning causes central nervous and respiratory depression of short duration, with vomiting, bradycardia, myoclonus and hypothermia; deaths are reported. [f:schep2012]

Blood concentrations of 80-100 mg/L cause inebriation and 300-500 mg/L may be fatal through respiratory depression and circulatory collapse. [f:busardo2015]

In an Australian series the deaths were mostly young men with unintentional overdose at home, with pulmonary oedema, pneumonia and aspiration of vomit at autopsy. [f:tay2022]

After prolonged use tolerance and dependence develop; withdrawal can last up to 21 days and be life-threatening, and benzodiazepines are the treatment of choice. [f:schep2012] [f:busardo2015]

## interactions

Co-ingestion of GHB or GBL with alcohol is associated with more severe toxicity, more intensive-care admissions and longer hospital stays. [f:tay2022]

The UBC guidelines add opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates and antihistamines to the combinations to avoid; GHB is a central nervous system depressant, so an additive effect is expected (an inference). [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines] [f:schep2012]

## counselling

Explain that the dose-effect curve is steep and the margin narrow; if the concentration of the liquid is uncertain the UBC guidelines advise a reduced dose and waiting before any further one, because overdoses often follow further doses. [f:busardo2015] [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines]

Ask about alcohol and other depressants: the UBC guidelines say never to combine alcohol and GHB, even at small doses, and warn about opioids (breathing), benzodiazepines, barbiturates and antihistamines. [f:tay2022] [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines]

Ask whether the person will be alone and where: control of the body can be lost, so a stable place to sit or lie down is needed and the UBC guidelines advise never using alone. [f:busardo2015] [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines]

Signs to recognise: deep sleep that cannot be interrupted, hard to tell from coma; vomiting while drowsy risks choking, so the airway must be kept clear. [f:busardo2015] [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines]

Ask about frequency: weekly use can bring sleep problems, anxiety and tremor, and large regular amounts physical dependence with withdrawal. [f:schep2012] [f:ubc-counselling-guidelines]
