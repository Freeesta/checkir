## molecule

Furanylfentanyl (Fu-F), N-phenyl-N-[1-(2-phenylethyl)piperidin-4-yl]furan-2-carboxamide. Base: C24H26N2O2, 374.5 g/mol, CAS 101345-66-8. Identity checked against PubChem and ChEBI: PubChem lists Fu-F as a synonym of this entry (main entry, no stereocentres). [f:pubchem] [f:chebi]

## category

Chemical class: fentanyl analogues (UNODC grouping). Expected effect: opioid. Typical role in samples: active principle. [f:unodc-ewa] [f:checkir-classification]

## origin

A synthetic substance, a fentanyl analogue. [f:armenian2018]

## metabolism

In human hepatocytes and in authentic urine from autopsy cases, the main metabolites of furanylfentanyl were formed by amide hydrolysis and dihydrodiol formation, while the nor-metabolite (N-dealkylation) was minor or not detected in case samples. [f:watanabe2017]

The dihydrodiol and amide hydrolysis metabolites were proposed as analytical targets in urine. [f:watanabe2017]

## effects

Fentanyl analogues are full agonists at the μ-opioid receptor; the typical clinical effects are miosis and respiratory and central nervous system depression. [f:armenian2018]

## doses

Potency varies widely between fentanyl analogues and no potency data for this compound were found in the sources used, so no dose is safe to suggest. [f:lovrecic2019]

## risks

In poisonings with fentanyl analogues larger naloxone doses are needed than in a classic heroin overdose. [f:lovrecic2019] [f:armenian2018]

As soon as a compound is regulated new ones appear, which makes detection difficult; some illicit pills with these compounds look like known opioids or benzodiazepines. [f:armenian2018]

## interactions

Benzodiazepines and other sedatives mixed with opioids are a risk factor for overdose. [f:kerensky2017]
