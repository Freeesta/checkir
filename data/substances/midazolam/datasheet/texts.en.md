## origin

A synthetic substance, a short-acting imidazobenzodiazepine. [f:pubchem] [f:peter2024]

## metabolism

Midazolam is hydroxylated by the CYP3A subfamily (CYP3A4 and CYP3A5; CYP3A7 is fetal); UGT 1A4, 2B4 and 2B7 then form 1'-hydroxymidazolam glucuronide, the main metabolite. Only 0.5% is excreted unchanged. [f:peter2024]

1'-Hydroxymidazolam binds the central benzodiazepine receptor with an affinity close to that of midazolam (2.2 versus 1.4 nmol/L); its half-life is about 1 hour, against 1.5-3.0 hours for midazolam. [f:peter2024]

Clearance and half-life differ by a factor of ≥10 between people; in cirrhosis the half-life rose from 3.8 to 7.4 hours. [f:peter2024]

## effects

Benzodiazepines act on the GABA-A receptor and produce sedation, hypnosis, anxiolysis, muscle relaxation, euphoria, amnesia and addiction. [f:el-balkhi2023]

They are central nervous system depressants and sedatives that can cause psychomotor impairment. [f:wu2023]

## risks

Relative potency is hard to establish, because it depends on the structure and on the many GABA-A receptor subtypes. [f:el-balkhi2023]

Serious cardiorespiratory complications, including deaths, and permanent neurological impairment have been documented with midazolam. [f:peter2024]

## interactions

Pharmacodynamic interactions with other central nervous system depressants, such as opioids, are extensively reported; pharmacokinetic interactions with opioids are considered less important. [f:el-balkhi2023]

Combined with other sedatives, the risk of overdose increases. [f:wu2023]

Potent CYP3A inhibitors raise the area under the midazolam concentration-time curve at least 10-fold. [f:peter2024]
