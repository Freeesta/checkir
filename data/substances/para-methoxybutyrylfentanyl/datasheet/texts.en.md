## molecule

para-Methoxybutyrylfentanyl (4-MeO-BF), N-(4-methoxyphenyl)-N-[1-(2-phenylethyl)piperidin-4-yl]butanamide. Formula C24H32N2O2, 380.5 g/mol. Identity checked on PubChem. [f:pubchem]

It is butyrylfentanyl with a methoxy group on the para position of the N-phenyl ring; butyrylfentanyl has its own sheet. [f:pubchem]

## category

Chemical class: fentanyl analogues (UNODC grouping). Expected effect: opioid. Typical role in samples: active principle. This classification is a checkir proposal awaiting review. [f:unodc-ewa] [f:checkir-classification]

## history

It was first reported in Europe in 2015 and later in the United States; the WHO critical review of 2018 notes very limited reports of its identification. [f:who-pmebf2018]

## origin

A synthetic substance, a fentanyl analogue; it has been found as a powder, a tablet and a nasal spray. [f:who-pmebf2018]

## effects

Fentanyl analogues are full agonists at the μ-opioid receptor; the typical clinical effects are miosis and respiratory and central nervous system depression. [f:armenian2018]

## doses

Potency varies widely between fentanyl analogues and no potency data for this compound were found in the sources used, so no dose is safe to suggest. [f:lovrecic2019]

## risks

In poisonings with fentanyl analogues larger naloxone doses are needed than in a classic heroin overdose. [f:lovrecic2019] [f:armenian2018]

The WHO review reports that it is a partial agonist at the μ-opioid receptor; acute intoxications showed reduced consciousness, respiratory depression and miosis, and naloxone was needed in some cases. [f:who-pmebf2018]

## interactions

Benzodiazepines and other sedatives mixed with opioids are a risk factor for overdose. [f:kerensky2017]
